Student Lecture

Author

Sadamori Kojaku

Published

August 17, 2026

Once in the semester you are the one teaching. You take a topic this course does not cover, spend twenty minutes making the class understand it, and make the room do something while you are up there.

Twenty minutes is short, on purpose. It is long enough for one idea and one activity, and too short for a survey. Pick a single question, answer it, and let the room find the answer with you.

Who You, alone.
How long 15 to 20 minutes, once in the semester.
What it is worth 10 points of your final grade. See how this course works.
What is required An interactive part. A talk with no activity does not count, however good the talk is.
How you claim one Post the topic id on Discord in #network-science. One student per topic, first post wins.
Before you teach Bring a one-page plan to office hours at least a week ahead. Your date is fixed when you claim the topic.

The one rule: the room has to do something

This is the part people get wrong, so it is worth being blunt about what counts.

Counts. A worksheet everyone fills in. A calculation the room does by hand on a graph small enough to fit on paper. A prediction the room commits to before you reveal the answer. Live coding where the class calls the shots. A physical demonstration the class takes part in.

Does not count. “Any questions?” A poll whose result changes nothing that follows. A demo the room only watches. A quiz at the end that tests whether they were listening.

The test is simple: if the class had not been there, would your twenty minutes have gone differently? If not, the activity is decoration.

TipThe activity is the hard half, not the easy half

Design it first and build the talk around it. Every topic below comes with a suggested activity for exactly that reason: it is the part that decides whether the lecture works, and it is the part people leave until the night before.

Whatever you design, do it yourself with a stopwatch first. An activity that takes you four minutes takes the room ten.

When you teach

Thirteen slots and thirteen of you, so everyone gets one and nobody doubles up. A slot is a normal class: your twenty minutes come out of the front of it and the day’s lecture carries on afterwards.

The dates are every teaching session from 17 September that is not a mini-project session, not a break and not a project presentation. That is what leaves exactly thirteen.

Two things worth noticing when you choose one. “That day’s lecture” is the material the room will have in its head that morning, so a lecture on the detectability limit lands harder on a Clustering day than on a Graph Embedding one. Nothing stops you pairing them differently, but say why if you do.

“Plan due” is the Friday office hour a clear week before your slot. One page: your question, your activity, and what you will cut if it runs long.

Picking a topic

Every topic below is adjacent to something you already know and not taught in this course. That is the whole design: you should be able to reach it with what modules 1 to 9 gave you, and nobody in the room should have heard it from me first.

“Start from” is a place to begin, not a reading list. One good paper plus the textbook chapter around it is usually the right amount.

Two of the groups split on a distinction worth having before you choose. Dynamics on a network holds the wiring still and asks what moves across it: a disease, an opinion, a phase. Dynamics of a network lets the wiring itself move: ties form, ties break, and the network you measured last month is not the one you have. Almost every real system does both at once, which is what the last topic in group C is about.

Thirty-five topics for a class of about thirteen, so nobody is left picking from scraps.

A. Networks this course never drew

Every network in this course had one kind of node, one kind of edge, no direction of time, no sign, and nothing inside it but nodes. Each of these relaxes one of those.

SL-01 · Bipartite networks and the projection trap
What happens to a network of actors and films when you throw the films away?
The room: projects a five-actor, three-film table by hand and counts the triangles the projection invented. Start from: Newman, Networks, on bipartite networks.

SL-02 · Signed networks and structural balance
Is “the enemy of my enemy is my friend” a theorem or a folk saying?
The room: signs the edges of a small graph, finds every unbalanced triangle, then repairs the graph with the fewest sign flips. Start from: Cartwright and Harary 1956; Leskovec, Huttenlocher and Kleinberg, “Signed networks in social media”, CHI 2010.

SL-03 · Beyond pairs: hypergraphs and simplicial complexes
Three people in a room is not three pairs. What does an edge list lose?
The room: writes a list of three-author papers in both encodings, then finds a question only one of them can answer. Start from: Battiston et al., “Networks beyond pairwise interactions”, Physics Reports 2020.

SL-04 · Multilayer networks: the same people on five platforms
Email, coffee and Slack are three networks over one set of people. What breaks when you flatten them into one?
The room: ranks the same six people by degree layer by layer, then on the flattened network, and finds the person the flattening hides. Start from: Kivela et al., “Multilayer networks”, Journal of Complex Networks 2014.

SL-05 · Networks of networks: when the grid and the internet take each other down
The power grid needs the control network, and the control network needs power. What does a single failure do to a pair like that?
The room: two small networks drawn side by side with dependency links between them; the room kills one node and traces the cascade back and forth until it stops. Start from: Buldyrev, Parshani, Paul, Stanley and Havlin, “Catastrophic cascade of failures in interdependent networks”, Nature 2010.

B. Dynamics on networks

The wiring holds still and something moves across it. Module 3 broke networks by removing nodes; these are about what happens on one while it is still standing.

SL-06 · The epidemic threshold
How infectious does a disease have to be before it takes off, and why does that depend on the hubs?
The room: runs an SIR round by hand with dice on a ten-node graph, once on a lattice and once with a hub added. Start from: Pastor-Satorras and Vespignani, “Epidemic spreading in scale-free networks”, PRL 2001.

SL-07 · Complex contagion: when one exposure is not enough
Why do rumours travel on long ties and behaviours do not?
The room: adopts under a two-neighbour rule on a ring lattice, then on the same lattice with shortcuts, and watches the shortcuts stop helping. Start from: Centola and Macy, “Complex contagions and the weakness of long ties”, AJS 2007.

SL-08 · Global cascades: the Watts threshold model
Why does one small shock do nothing and the next one, identical to it, take down everything?
The room: each student is a node with a threshold on a card; run the cascade twice from two different seeds. Start from: Watts, “A simple model of global cascades on random networks”, PNAS 2002.

SL-09 · Opinion dynamics: the voter model
If everyone copies a random neighbour forever, what does the network end up believing?
The room: coloured cards, one per student, five rounds of copy-a-neighbour; count how long consensus takes on a line against a clique. Start from: Castellano, Fortunato and Loreto, “Statistical physics of social dynamics”, Reviews of Modern Physics 2009.

SL-10 · Synchronization: Kuramoto on a network
Fireflies, power grids and clapping audiences all lock into step. What structure makes that easy?
The room: claps, first with everyone listening to everyone, then with each person listening to two neighbours only. Time both. Start from: Arenas, Diaz-Guilera, Kurths, Moreno and Zhou, “Synchronization in complex networks”, Physics Reports 2008.

SL-11 · Evolutionary games: why cooperation survives on a lattice
Defection wins every well-mixed prisoner’s dilemma. Put the players on a network and it stops winning. Why?
The room: plays three rounds on a five-by-five paper grid, copying whichever neighbour scored best, and watches clusters of cooperators hold their edges. Start from: Nowak and May, “Evolutionary games and spatial chaos”, Nature 1992; Santos and Pacheco, PRL 2005.

C. Dynamics of networks

Now the wiring is the thing that moves. Ties form, ties break, and the network you measured last month is not the one you have.

SL-12 · Temporal networks: paths that respect the clock
A path exists in the aggregated network. Can anything actually travel it?
The room: gets a table of timestamped contacts and works out who could have infected whom, and who could not. Start from: Holme and Saramaki, “Temporal networks”, Physics Reports 2012.

SL-13 · Graphs over time: densification and shrinking diameters
Everyone assumes a growing network keeps its average degree. Measured, it does not, and the diameter goes the wrong way too.
The room: gets three snapshots of a growing network, plots edges against nodes on log-log paper, reads the exponent off the slope, and predicts what happened to the diameter. Start from: Leskovec, Kleinberg and Faloutsos, “Graphs over time: densification laws, shrinking diameters and possible explanations”, KDD 2005.

SL-14 · Fitness: why the first mover does not always win
Preferential attachment says the oldest node wins. Google was not the first search engine.
The room: grows two networks side by side on the board, one with fitness and one without, six steps each, and compares who ends up the hub. Start from: Bianconi and Barabasi, “Competition and multiscaling in evolving networks”, EPL 2001.

SL-15 · Adaptive networks: when the structure and the state rewire each other
Real people do not only copy their neighbours. They also drop the neighbours they disagree with. What does that change?
The room: reruns the voter model with one extra move, cutting and rewiring a tie on disagreement, and watches consensus turn into two silent camps. Start from: Holme and Newman, “Nonequilibrium phase transition in the coevolution of networks and opinions”, PRE 2006; Gross and Blasius, “Adaptive coevolutionary networks: a review”, J. R. Soc. Interface 2008.

D. Controlling a network

Nothing in this course ever tried to steer a network. This is the part of the field that does, and it turns out to be a question about matchings.

SL-16 · Controllability: which nodes do you have to grab?
You may inject a signal at a few nodes. How few, and which ones, before you can drive the whole system anywhere you like?
The room: finds a maximum matching by hand on a small directed graph, reads the unmatched nodes off as the drivers, then adds one edge and watches the driver count change. Start from: Liu, Slotine and Barabasi, “Controllability of complex networks”, Nature 2011.

SL-17 · Observability: where do you put the sensors?
The mirror image. You cannot measure every node, so which handful tells you the state of all the others?
The room: places two sensors on a drawn network, marks every node whose state can be reconstructed from them, then tries to do better with the same two. Start from: Liu, Slotine and Barabasi, “Observability of complex systems”, PNAS 2013.

E. Structure beyond communities, and how to test for it

Module 5 asked “which group is this node in?” Not every network is made of groups, and a pattern you can see is not yet a pattern that is there.

SL-18 · Network motifs
Which small subgraphs appear more often than chance, and what does that say about the system?
The room: counts all three-node patterns in a small directed graph by hand, then in a rewired copy, and compares. Start from: Milo et al., “Network motifs: simple building blocks of complex networks”, Science 2002.

SL-19 · Exponential random graph models
How do you fit a statistical model to a network when you only have one of them?
The room: proposes statistics for a small friendship graph, then argues about which set would be enough to reproduce it. Start from: Robins, Pattison, Kalish and Lusher, “An introduction to exponential random graph (p*) models”, Social Networks 2007.

SL-20 · Core-periphery structure
What if a network has no communities at all, but a dense middle and a loose edge?
The room: gets a shuffled adjacency matrix and reorders the rows by hand until the core is visible. Start from: Borgatti and Everett, “Models of core/periphery structures”, Social Networks 2000.

SL-21 · Node roles: within-module degree and participation
Two nodes have the same degree and sit in the same community. Are they doing the same job?
The room: computes both coordinates for six labelled nodes and places each on the role diagram. Start from: Guimera and Amaral, “Functional cartography of complex metabolic networks”, Nature 2005.

SL-22 · How many communities? The detectability limit
Below a certain contrast no algorithm can find the communities. Not a hard problem: impossible.
The room: splits two planted-partition graphs on paper, one above the threshold and one below, and finds out which was which. Start from: Decelle, Krzakala, Moore and Zdeborova, “Inference and phase transitions in the detection of modules in sparse networks”, PRL 2011.

SL-23 · Navigability: Kleinberg’s exponent and greedy routing
Module 2 said short paths exist. Milgram’s letters had to find them without a map. How?
The room: routes a message across a printed grid with long-range shortcuts, forwarding greedily, and counts hops against its own guess. Start from: Kleinberg, “Navigation in a small world”, Nature 2000.

F. Learning and prediction on graphs

Modules 8 and 9 built embeddings and GNNs. These are the questions that arrive the moment you try to use one.

SL-24 · Link prediction without learning, and how to evaluate it honestly
Common neighbours is one line of code. When does it beat an embedding, and how would you know?
The room: ranks candidate pairs by common neighbours for three hidden edges, then sees what a random split hid from it. Start from: Liben-Nowell and Kleinberg, “The link-prediction problem for social networks”, JASIST 2007.

SL-25 · Knowledge graphs and TransE
What changes when an edge has a type, and the type is part of what you embed?
The room: does the vector arithmetic for three facts by hand and predicts a fourth. Start from: Bordes et al., “Translating embeddings for modeling multi-relational data”, NeurIPS 2013.

SL-26 · Over-squashing: the bottleneck deep GNNs cannot pass
Over-smoothing is not the only reason deep GNNs fail. What else breaks?
The room: counts how many nodes get squeezed into one vector across a bridge, on a drawn graph, at two, three and four hops. Start from: Alon and Yahav, “On the bottleneck of graph neural networks and its practical implications”, ICLR 2021.

SL-27 · Explaining a GNN’s prediction
The model says fraud. Which edges made it say so?
The room: proposes the subgraph that explains a prediction, then checks it by deleting edges and re-running the aggregation by hand. Start from: Ying et al., “GNNExplainer: generating explanations for graph neural networks”, NeurIPS 2019.

G. Seeing a network

Every figure in this course was a drawing somebody chose. These are about the choosing, and about when to stop drawing nodes and edges altogether.

SL-28 · Force-directed layout: what the picture is actually optimizing
Springs and repulsion produce a picture. What does a short edge in it mean, and why do two runs disagree?
The room: everyone draws the same twelve-edge graph by hand in sixty seconds; compare a dozen drawings of one network before looking at what the algorithm minimizes. Start from: Fruchterman and Reingold, “Graph drawing by force-directed placement”, Software: Practice and Experience 1991; Kobourov, “Force-directed drawing algorithms”, Handbook of Graph Drawing and Visualization 2013.

SL-29 · The hairball, and what to do instead
At a few thousand edges the node-link picture stops answering questions. What replaces it?
The room: gets the same network as a hairball and as an aggregated or bundled drawing, answers one question from each, and reports which was answerable at all. Start from: Holten, “Hierarchical edge bundles”, IEEE TVCG 2006.

SL-30 · Node-link or matrix: which picture answers which question
The hairball is not always the wrong drawing either. When is a matrix better?
The room: same network, two printouts; time it on “are these two nodes connected?” and on “find the densest group”. Start from: Ghoniem, Fekete and Castagliola, “A comparison of the readability of graphs using node-link and matrix-based representations”, InfoVis 2004.

H. Networks meet data and people

Where the network came from, and what it does once it is pointed at people.

SL-31 · Networks built from correlations
Brain and gene networks are usually thresholded correlation matrices. What structure does that invent?
The room: correlates four short series by hand, thresholds at two values, and watches the triangle count move. Start from: Zalesky, Fornito and Bullmore, “On the use of correlation as a measure of network connectivity”, NeuroImage 2012.

SL-32 · Weighted networks: which edges are real?
Every pair has some weight. How do you throw away the noise without throwing away the small nodes?
The room: prunes one weighted graph twice, once with a global threshold and once locally, and compares what survives at a low-degree node. Start from: Serrano, Boguna and Vespignani, “Extracting the multiscale backbone of complex weighted networks”, PNAS 2009.

SL-33 · De-anonymizing a social graph
The names were removed. Why is that not anonymity?
The room: gets an unlabelled graph plus one labelled fragment and re-identifies nodes from degree and neighbourhood alone. Start from: Narayanan and Shmatikov, “De-anonymizing social networks”, IEEE Symposium on Security and Privacy 2009.

SL-34 · Ranking, recommendation and the glass ceiling
“People you may know” is a link predictor. What does running it for years do to a minority group?
The room: runs three rounds of a recommend-the-most-connected rule on a small mixed graph and watches the shares diverge. Start from: Karimi, Genois, Wagner, Singer and Strohmaier, “Homophily influences ranking of minorities in social networks”, Scientific Reports 2018.

SL-35 · How false news spreads
True and false stories travel the same network. Why do they not travel the same way?
The room: predicts which of six cascade shapes came from true stories, commits to an answer, and then sees it. Start from: Vosoughi, Roy and Aral, “The spread of true and false news online”, Science 2018.

Propose your own

Bring one. It has to pass the two tests every topic above passes:

  1. It is not in this course. Check the module pages first. PageRank, Louvain and over-smoothing are already taught, so a lecture on them is a review.
  2. You can name the activity in one sentence, and it fits inside the twenty minutes along with the talk.

Propose it on Discord the way you would claim one from the list, and say what the room will be doing.

What a good one looks like

  • One question, stated in the first minute and answered before the end. Twenty minutes will hold one idea properly or four badly.
  • The activity comes early, not as an afterthought at the end. Let the room hit the difficulty before you resolve it. That is how every module here is built, and it is why the pen-and-paper exercise comes before the lecture rather than after it.
  • One worked example, small enough to see. Six nodes on a slide beat a thousand-node hairball for anything you actually want to explain.
  • You say what you did not cover. Naming the boundary of your twenty minutes is a sign that you know where it is.
