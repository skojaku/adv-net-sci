{\LARGE\bfseries Module 5 --- Pitch your partition}\hfill{\color{softgrey}\small 30 minutes, in groups}

\vspace{0.2em}
\rule{\textwidth}{0.8pt}

\textbf{The situation.} The lecturer has drawn where everyone in this room sits. Two people
who sit close are joined by an edge. That is a network of about 15 people. Nobody knows
which communities it ``really'' has: there is no answer key. Your group runs one method on
it and has three minutes to say why its partition is the one to use. You may argue only
from your method's own definition of a community.

\vspace{0.3em}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.3}
\begin{tabular}{@{}>{\bfseries}c >{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{2.7cm} p{12.2cm}@{}}
1 & Shape &
A community is a set of people linked to one another through the graph, with nobody
outside linked to them. The groups are the connected pieces; the notebook also lists the
cliques (sets in which everyone is joined to everyone). \\
2 & Cut &
A community is one side of a split into two. The split with the smallest
$\text{Cut}(V_1,V_2)=\sum_{i\in V_1}\sum_{j\in V_2}A_{ij}$ divided by a size term
(ratio cut, normalized cut) wins. \\
3 & Chance: modularity &
A community is a set with more edges inside than a random network with the same degrees
would put there. Maximize $Q=\frac{1}{2m}\sum_{ij}\bigl[A_{ij}-\frac{k_ik_j}{2m}\bigr]\delta(c_i,c_j)$
with Leiden or Louvain. \\
4 & Pattern: block model (graph-tool) &
A community is a set of people who connect to every group in the same way. The grouping
that gives the shortest description of the network wins, and a group is kept only if it
pays for itself. \\
\end{tabular}

\vspace{0.3em}
\textbf{The graph is a choice too.} \emph{$k$-nearest-neighbour graph}: each person is
joined to their $k$ nearest people. \emph{$\varepsilon$-neighbourhood graph}: two people are
joined when their distance is at most $\varepsilon$ seat widths. Your group may change the
graph in the notebook. If you do, say so in your pitch.

\medskip
{\large\bfseries Plan}\par
\vspace{-0.3em}\rule{\textwidth}{0.4pt}

\begin{tabular}{@{}>{\bfseries}r p{1.9cm} p{12.6cm}@{}}
5 min & Prepare &
Write four sentences: (a) your definition of a community; (b) who your partition puts
together, and who it separates from whom; (c) one number from the output that supports
it; (d) one thing it cannot do. The other groups will find (d) if you do not. \\
12 min & Pitch &
Three minutes per group, in the order 1, 2, 3, 4. Everyone else writes down one question
for the group that is speaking. \\
6 min & Questions &
Each group asks its question. The answer must come from the answering group's own
definition. Before you call another group's partition wrong, say whose definition you are
using. \\
5 min & Compare &
Run the last cell of the notebook: all four partitions side by side, and how much each
pair agrees. \\
\end{tabular}

\medskip
{\large\bfseries Questions to ask}\par
\vspace{-0.3em}\rule{\textwidth}{0.4pt}

\begin{itemize}
\item What did you decide before running it ($\varepsilon$ or $k$, the objective, the seed)? What changes if you decide differently?
\item Pick a person who sits between two groups. Why is that person with the group they are in?
\item Run it again with another seed or setting. Is the answer the same?
\item Does your method need to be told how many groups there are?
\item Which of the four would you use to form discussion groups for this class? Does the answer depend on what the groups are for?
\end{itemize}
