% Break the page unless #1 of it is left.
%
% This is needspace's glue trick, written out because needspace.sty lives in
% texlive-latex-extra and not in a BasicTeX install. Do NOT rewrite it as a test
% on \pagegoal minus \pagetotal: the page builder has not necessarily run when
% the macro expands, so those numbers can still be the previous page's, the test
% says "no room" on a page that is empty, and the \newpage throws that page
% away. m02 shipped a blank page 3 that way, and it is invisible in the source.
\newcommand{\needroom}[1]{%
  \par
  \begingroup
    \setlength{\dimen0}{#1}%
    \vskip 0pt plus \dimen0
    \penalty -100%
    \vskip 0pt plus -\dimen0
  \endgroup}

\parthead{1}{Guessing by eye}

{\bf Question 1(a)}: Estimate the ratio of the big shape to the small one in
each pair --- length, area, area, volume.

\arrow \ans{4, 4, 4, 8.} Twice the side or radius gives four times the area;
twice the edge gives eight times the volume.

Typical guesses are about 2.5 for the two areas and 3.5 for the cube. Only the
length pair is usually estimated correctly.

{\bf Question 1(b)}: Which pair did you and your neighbour disagree on most?

\arrow \ans{The cube.} The error grows with the number of dimensions.

{\bf Question 3}: What can you read about the four values above 5? What does
the chart fail to show?

\arrow \ans{Almost nothing.} The remaining bars are 3, 0, 0, 1. The chart does
not show that three of the four lie between 6 and 10, and a bar of height 1 is
hard to tell from 0. \ans{80\% of the data sits in one bin}, so the chart
resolves the bulk and not the tail.

\parthead{3}{Bins that double}

{\bf Question 4}: Bin the same values into six bins, each twice as wide as the
one before.

\arrow \ans{4, 8, 4, 2, 1, 1} (above right). The eight values of 2 go in the
1--2 bin by the boundary rule. The total is still 20.

{\bf Question 5}: Largest bar, and a comparison of the two charts.

\arrow \ans{8}, the 1--2 bin, and \ans{all six bars are resolved}. After the
peak \ans{each bar is half the one before}: 8, 4, 2, 1. The same halving is
present in Part 2's data but not resolvable at width 5.

{\bf Question 6(a)}: Mark 5 on each ruler.

\arrow Below. Ruler A: at $5/32$ of its length. Ruler B: between 4 and 8 and
\ans{left of the midpoint}, since the midpoint of a doubling step is
$4\sqrt{2} \approx 5.7$.

\begin{center}
\resizebox{\textwidth}{!}{\rulers{\fivemarks}}
\end{center}

{\bf Question 6(b)}: What fraction of each ruler spans 1 to 4? Why can Ruler B
have no 0?

\arrow Ruler A: $3/32$, \ans{about 9\%}, for 80\% of the data. Ruler B: two
steps out of five, \ans{40\%}. Ruler B is the $x$-axis of the Question 4
chart, and a plot drawn on it is a \ans{log plot}.

Its left end is reached by repeated halving, so \ans{0 lies at $-\infty$}.
Zero and negative values cannot be plotted on a log axis.

{\bf Question 7}: Effect of one added value of 12, then of 2.

\arrow 12 falls in the 8--16 bin: $1 \to 2$, \ans{+100\%}. 2 falls in the
1--2 bin: $8 \to 9$, \ans{+12.5\%}.

\ans{The left bars are stable, the right bars are not}, and the right bars
carry the tail. Wider bins cut the variance and the resolution together;
narrower bins put single counts back in the tail. \ans{No bin width removes the
trade-off.}

{\bf Question 9(a)}: Plot the five fractions on doubling axes.

\arrow \ans{A straight line.} Each doubling of $k$ halves the count: 16, 8, 4,
2, 1, and on log--log axes halving one variable as the other doubles is a line
of \ans{slope $-1$}.

This count is the \ans{CCDF}, $P(X > k)$. It needs no bins.

\begin{center}
\resizebox{0.85\textwidth}{!}{\ccdfgrid{\ccdfsol}}
\end{center}

{\bf Question 9(b)}: The fraction of values at most $k$: 0.80, 0.90, 0.95.

\arrow They \ans{rise towards 1 and flatten}, and cannot exceed it. Both counts
carry the same data, since one is 1 minus the other, but this one compresses
the whole tail into $[0.95, 1]$.

\ans{The CCDF shows the value 24}, as its rightmost point. The ``at most $k$''
count is the \ans{CDF}: use it for the bulk and the CCDF for the tail.

{\bf Question 10}: Procedure for $10^6$ nodes, and which plot to publish.

\arrow \ans{For each $k$, count the nodes of degree greater than $k$, divide by
$n$, and plot against $k$ on log--log axes.} One pass over the degrees, no bin
width, no empty bins, and every node contributes to every point.

\ans{Publish the third plot.} The first resolves only the bulk; the second is
noisy where the data is thin; the third resolves the tail.

Names: the number of friends of a node is its \ans{degree}, the counts are the
\ans{degree distribution}, and a degree distribution with a straight-line CCDF
on log--log axes is a \ans{power law}. If $p(k) \sim k^{-\gamma}$ then the CCDF
has slope $1 - \gamma$, so the slope of $-1$ here means
$\mans{\gamma = 2}$.

A straight line on log--log axes is not proof of a power law; the claim needs a
statistical fit, not an eye.
