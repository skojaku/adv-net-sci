% Break the page unless #1 of it is left.
%
% This is needspace's glue trick, written out because needspace.sty lives in
% texlive-latex-extra and not in a BasicTeX install. Do NOT rewrite it as a test
% on \pagegoal minus \pagetotal: the page builder has not necessarily run when
% the macro expands, so those numbers can still be the previous page's, the test
% says "no room" on a page that is empty, and the \newpage throws that page
% away. m02 shipped a blank page 3 that way, and it is invisible in the source.
\newcommand{\needroom}[1]{%
  \par
  \begingroup
    \setlength{\dimen0}{#1}%
    \vskip 0pt plus \dimen0
    \penalty -100%
    \vskip 0pt plus -\dimen0
  \endgroup}

\parthead{1}{Guessing by eye}

{\bf Question 1(a)}: For each of the four pairs, how many times bigger is the
big one? Length for the lines, area for the square and the circle, volume for
the cube.

\arrow \ans{4, 4, 4, and 8.} The line is four times as long. The big square has
twice the side, so \ans{four times the area}. The big circle has twice the
radius, so \ans{four times the area}. The big cube has twice the edge, so
\ans{eight times the volume}.

Most people get the line right and underestimate the other three. Typical
guesses are about 2.5 for the two areas and about 3.5 for the cube.

{\bf Question 1(b)}: On which pair were you and your neighbour furthest apart,
and why that pair?

\arrow \ans{The cube.} Guesses get worse as the number of directions a shape
grows in goes up.

{\bf Question 3}: Put your thumb over the tallest bar. What can you still say
about the four people who watched more than five hours? And what does a
newspaper reader take away in two seconds?

\arrow \ans{Very little.} What is left is 3, 0, 0, 1. You cannot see that three
of the four watched between 6 and 10 hours, and the last bar is one person
high.

The reader concludes \ans{``nearly everybody watches under five hours''}. That
is true, and it is the least useful fact in the data. The chart does not show
the person who watched 24 hours.

\parthead{3}{Bins that double}

{\bf Question 4}: The same twenty numbers, counted into six bins, each twice as
wide as the one before.

\arrow \ans{4, 8, 4, 2, 1, 1}, drawn above right. The eight people on exactly
2 hours go in the 1--2 bin, by the sheet's boundary rule. The total is still
twenty; only the bins changed.

{\bf Question 5}: In Part 2 sixteen of the twenty sat in one bar. What is the
biggest bar now, and what did the doubling bins do?

\arrow The biggest bar is \ans{8}, the 1--2 bin. \ans{All six bars are
visible.} After the peak, \ans{each bar is about half the one before} --- 8, 4,
2, 1. Part 2's chart holds the same halving but has no room to show it.

{\bf Question 6(a)}: Mark where 5 goes on each ruler.

\arrow Below. On Ruler A, 5 sits just past 4, about one sixth of the way along.
On Ruler B it sits between 4 and 8, slightly \ans{left} of the middle of that
gap: the middle of a doubling step is about 5.7, not 6.

{\bf Question 6(b)}: Sixteen of your twenty watched between 1 and 4 hours. How
much of Ruler A do they get? How much of Ruler B? And why can Ruler B never be
given a 0?

\arrow On Ruler A, 1 to 4 is three units out of thirty-two ---
\ans{about a tenth} of the ruler, for 80\% of the people. On Ruler B it is two
steps out of five, \ans{40\%}. \ans{Ruler B is the x-axis of the doubling-bin
chart in Question 4}, and a chart drawn on it is called a \ans{log plot}.

Ruler B has no 0 because its left end is reached by halving: 1, $\tfrac12$,
$\tfrac14$, and so on. \ans{Zero is infinitely far to the left.} A value that
can be zero or negative cannot go on a log axis.

\parthead{4}{Counting without bins}

{\bf Question 7}: One more person watched 12 hours --- which bar of Part 3
changes, and by what fraction of itself? Then one more who watched 2 hours
instead. Which bars would you trust?

\arrow The 12-hour person falls in the 8--16 bin, which goes from 1 to 2:
\ans{+100\%}. The 2-hour person falls in the 1--2 bin, which goes from 8 to 9:
\ans{+12.5\%}.

So \ans{the tall bars on the left are stable and the short bars on the right
are not}. The right-hand bars are the ones you want, because they hold the
heavy watchers. Wider bins cut the noise but lose the detail; narrower bins put
one person back in each tail bar. \ans{No bin width fixes this}, which is why
Part 4 stops choosing one.

{\bf Question 9(a)}: Plot the five fractions on two doubling rulers. What do
the points do?

\arrow \ans{They fall on a straight line.}

\begin{center}
\resizebox{0.66\textwidth}{!}{\ccdfgrid{\ccdfsol}}
\end{center}

Each time $k$ doubles, \ans{the count halves}: 16, 8, 4, 2, 1. On two doubling
rulers, one quantity halving as the other doubles is a straight line at 45
degrees.

This count is the \ans{CCDF}, the fraction of the data above $k$. It needs no
bins.

{\bf Question 9(b)}: Someone counts the other way round: the fraction watching
$k$ hours \emph{or fewer}, which is 0.80 at $k = 4$, 0.90 at $k = 8$, 0.95 at
$k = 16$. What are those numbers doing, and which count shows you the 24-hour
person?

\arrow They \ans{rise towards 1 and flatten}, and cannot pass it. The two
counts hold the same twenty numbers, since one is 1 minus the other. This one
puts every heavy watcher between 0.95 and 1, where the differences are too
small to read.

\ans{The ``more than'' count shows the 24-hour person}, as the last point on
the right. The ``or fewer'' count is the \ans{CDF}. Use the CDF for the typical
case and the CCDF for the tail.

{\bf Question 10}: The twenty people are now twenty nodes and each number is
how many friends that node has. How would you draw Question 9(a)'s picture for
a million nodes? And which of today's three pictures would you print?

\arrow For each $k$, \ans{count how many nodes have more than $k$ friends,
divide by a million, and plot that against $k$ with both axes doubling}.
Nothing in the procedure grows with the network except the counting: no bin
width, no empty bars, and every node contributes to every point.

\ans{Print the third picture.} The first hides 80\% of the network in one bar.
The second shows the shape but moves by 100\% where the data is thin. The third
shows the tail.

Names for what you drew. How many friends a node has is its \ans{degree}. The
counts are the \ans{degree distribution}. A degree distribution whose CCDF is a
straight line on doubling axes is a \ans{power law}, and its slope says how
rare the hubs are --- here the count halves at every doubling, a slope of
$\mans{-1}$.

One caveat. A straight line on a log plot is not proof of a power law. Testing
the claim needs statistics, not eyeballing.
