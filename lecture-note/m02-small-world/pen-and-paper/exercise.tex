% Break the page unless #1 of it is left. This is the one macro this sheet
% wants out of needspace.sty, written out because needspace is in
% texlive-latex-extra and not in a BasicTeX install, which is what the sheets
% get built on by hand.
%
% It is needspace's glue trick, not a test on \pagegoal. Comparing \pagegoal
% with \pagetotal by hand looks like the obvious way to write this and is
% wrong: the page builder has not necessarily run by the time the macro is
% expanded, so the figures it reads can still be the previous page's, the test
% says "no room" on a page that is empty, and \newpage throws away the page
% that was about to be filled. That cost this sheet a blank page 3. Here the
% stretchable skip lets the page break at the penalty only when the room is
% genuinely short, and the second skip takes the glue back out.
\newcommand{\needroom}[1]{%
  \par
  \begingroup
    \setlength{\dimen0}{#1}%
    \vskip 0pt plus \dimen0
    \penalty -100%
    \vskip 0pt plus -\dimen0
  \endgroup}

% A part heading. It carries the break test, because a heading printed at the
% foot of a page with nothing under it is worse than a short page.
\newcommand{\parthead}[2]{%
  \needroom{6\baselineskip}%
  \partheadhere{#1}{#2}}

% The same heading with no break test, for the one that is already inside a

  \vspace{0.1em}}

\parthead{1}{Ringville}

Sixteen people live in Ringville and they sit in one circle. Everybody is
friends with the two on their left and the two on their right: four friends
each, thirty-two friendships in the town.

\vspace{-0.4em}
\begin{center}
\resizebox{0.53\textwidth}{!}{\ringtown{}{\dfill{1}{0}\dfill{2}{1}}}
\end{center}
\vspace{-0.5em}

{\bf Question 1(a)}: Person 1 has a letter for person 9. Trace the chain on the
drawing. How many handshakes does it take?

{\bf Question 1(b)}: Now everybody. In each person's box on the drawing, write
how many handshakes it takes to reach them from person 1. Two are written in
already, and you may find you only have to work out half of the rest.

\needroom{3\baselineskip}%
{\bf Question 1(c)}: Add up every number in the boxes. What is the total?

Share that total out among the fifteen other people. That is the town's
\textbf{average}.

And what is the biggest number in any box --- the town's \textbf{worst case}?

\needroom{4\baselineskip}%
{\bf Question 1(d)}: A newspaper wants one number for a headline: ``in
Ringville, two people are X handshakes apart.''

The post office wants one number for a promise it prints on the postbox:
``every letter arrives in X handshakes or fewer.''

You have two numbers, the average and the worst case. Which one goes in the
headline, and which one goes on the postbox?

What goes wrong if you swap them?

\needroom{3\baselineskip}%
{\bf Question 2}: Ringville grows. A thousand people, still one circle, still
four friends each.

How many handshakes does it take to reach the person sitting directly opposite
you?

And if the circle held all eight billion people on Earth?

Could a world wired like Ringville pass a letter from Omaha to Boston in six
handshakes? Why?

\parthead{2}{Two shortcuts}

Two of the sixteen went to college and came home with a friend from the far
side of town: person 1 is now also friends with person 9, and person 5 with
person 13 --- thirty-four friendships, not thirty-two.

\vspace{-0.4em}
\begin{center}
\resizebox{0.53\textwidth}{!}{\ringtown{\shortcuts}{\dfill{1}{0}\dfill{9}{1}}}
\end{center}
\vspace{-0.5em}

{\bf Question 3}: Write the handshake counts into the boxes again, this time for
the town with the two new friendships. Two are written in already.

Then work out the same three numbers as in 1(c): the new total, the new
average, and the new worst case.

\needroom{3\baselineskip}%
{\bf Question 4}: Thirty-two friendships became thirty-four. Work out both
changes as percentages: by how much did the number of friendships change, and by
how much did the average change?

Say, in your own words, what those two new friendships did that thirty-two
ordinary ones could not.

\parthead{3}{Do your friends know each other?}

\begin{minipage}[c]{0.44\textwidth}

Ringville again, as it was in Part 1, before the two shortcuts. Take somebody

Four people (1, 2, 4, 5) can be paired up six ways: (1,\,2), (1,\,4),
(1,\,5), (2,\,4), (2,\,5), (4,\,5). Those six pairs are the dashed lines.

Darken the dashed lines that are real friendships.

\vspace{0.3em}

{\bf Question 5(a)}: Ringville grows to ten thousand people, still four friends
each. How many of person 3's six pairs are friends now?

\needroom{3\baselineskip}%
{\bf Question 5(b)}: A different town: the same sixteen people and the same
thirty-two friendships, but this time each friendship is drawn between two
people uniformly at random.

Pick two people. What is the chance that they are friends?

Each of person 3's six pairs is friends with that same chance. So out of six
pairs, how many would you expect to be friends?

And in a random town of ten thousand, still four friends each?

\needroom{3\baselineskip}%
{\bf Question 5(c)}: Take the number of person 3's six pairs that really are
friendships, and divide it by six. That fraction is person 3's
\textbf{local clustering}. You have now worked it out four
times: Ringville, Ringville with ten thousand people, and the two random towns.

Compare the four numbers. What is it about the way friends are made in each of
the two towns that decides whether local clustering survives when the town
grows?

Three people who are all friends with each other make a \textbf{triangle}.
Ringville, before the two shortcuts, has sixteen of them.

{\bf Question 6(a)}: Now go back to the drawing in Part 2, where the two
shortcuts have gone in.

Do persons 1 and 9 have a friend in common? Do persons 5 and 13?

So how many triangles does the town have now?

\needroom{3\baselineskip}%
{\bf Question 6(b)}: In Part 2 the two shortcuts were new friendships. The real
model never adds one: it takes a friendship away and puts it back somewhere
else.

Do that once: person 1 stops being friends with person 2, and becomes friends
with person 9 instead. The town still has thirty-two friendships.

Which friends did persons 1 and 2 have in common?

So how many triangles does the town lose?

\needroom{3\baselineskip}%
{\bf Question 6(c)}: What does a shortcut buy the town, and what does it cost?
