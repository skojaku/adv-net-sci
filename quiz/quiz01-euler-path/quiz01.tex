\question{1}{4}{Even degrees are not enough.}

Let's consider an undirected graph in which every node has even degree. One may
argue that an Euler path must exist. This is false. Why?

\vspace{0.2em}

\textbf{(a) [3 pts]} Give an example graph in which every node has even degree
but no Euler path exists. Your graph must have at most 6 nodes, and at least
one edge at every node. Name every node and give its degree.

\vspace{0.2em}

\textbf{(b) [1 pt]} Apart from the requirement on the nodes' degrees, what is
the additional requirement for a graph to have an Euler path? Explain why this
condition is needed.


% ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
\question{2}{6}{Now the edges have directions.}

Now let's consider a directed graph, where a walk may only follow the arrow on
each edge. An Euler path in a directed graph is a walk that uses every directed
edge exactly once.

\vspace{0.2em}

\textbf{(a) [3 pts]} Write the condition under which a directed graph has an
Euler path. Use the words \emph{in-degree} (the number of edges pointing to a
node) and \emph{out-degree} (the number pointing away from it), and do not
forget the condition that has nothing to do with degrees.

\vspace{0.2em}

\textbf{(b) [2 pts]} Give a directed graph with 4 or 5 nodes that \textbf{has}
an Euler path. Name every node, give every edge its direction, and say where
the walk starts and where it ends.

\vspace{0.2em}

\textbf{(c) [1 pt]} Give a directed graph with 4 or 5 nodes that has
\textbf{no} Euler path. Then name the node or nodes that break your condition,
and give their in-degree and out-degree.
