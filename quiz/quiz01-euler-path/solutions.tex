\question{1}{4}{Even degrees are not enough.}

\textbf{(a) [3 pts]} Two separate triangles. Every node has degree $2$, which
is even, and every node has an edge. There is still no Euler path, because no
walk can cross from the left triangle to the right one.

\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}
  \node[gnode] (A) at (0,1.1) {A};
  \node[gnode] (B) at (-0.95,-0.55) {B};
  \node[gnode] (C) at (0.95,-0.55) {C};
  \draw[gedge] (A) -- (B) -- (C) -- (A);
  \node[glabel, above=1mm of A] {2};
  \node[glabel, left=1mm of B] {2};
  \node[glabel, right=1mm of C] {2};

  \node[gnode] (D) at (5.4,1.1) {D};
  \node[gnode] (E) at (4.45,-0.55) {E};
  \node[gnode] (F) at (6.35,-0.55) {F};
  \draw[gedge] (D) -- (E) -- (F) -- (D);
  \node[glabel, above=1mm of D] {2};
  \node[glabel, left=1mm of E] {2};
  \node[glabel, right=1mm of F] {2};
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}

\textbf{(b) [1 pt]} The missing requirement is \textbf{connectivity}. All the
edges have to sit in one connected piece. An Euler path is a single unbroken
walk, and a walk cannot jump between components, so it can only ever cover the
edges of the component it starts in.

\begin{grading}
3 pts for any disconnected graph with all-even degrees and no isolated nodes:
two triangles, two squares, a triangle and a square, two disjoint 4-cycles.
1 pt for the word \emph{connected} or \emph{connectivity}, with or without the
sentence. Watch for the near-miss: two triangles \textbf{sharing one node} is
connected, has all even degrees, and \emph{does} have an Euler circuit ---
that is not a counterexample, so no credit for (a), though (b) may still earn
its point.
\end{grading}

\vspace{0.3em}

{\small\textbf{Bonus (ungraded).} One edge. Join any node of the first triangle
to any node of the second. The two joined nodes become degree $3$, so the graph
now has exactly two odd nodes and is connected --- an Euler path exists, and it
must start at one of those two nodes and end at the other.}

\question{2}{6}{Now the edges have directions.}

\textbf{(a) [3 pts]} A directed graph has an Euler path exactly when both of
these hold.

\begin{enumerate}[label=\arabic*., leftmargin=1.8em, itemsep=0.2em, topsep=0.2em]
  \item \textbf{Connectivity.} Ignore the arrows. Every node that has at least
        one edge lies in one single connected piece.
  \item \textbf{Degrees.} Either every node has in-degree $=$ out-degree
        (then the walk is a closed \emph{circuit} and may start anywhere), or
        exactly one node has out-degree $=$ in-degree $+\,1$ (the start),
        exactly one node has in-degree $=$ out-degree $+\,1$ (the end), and
        every other node has in-degree $=$ out-degree.
\end{enumerate}

The arrows into and out of a node have to pair up, because the walk enters and
leaves every time it passes through. Only the start and the end are allowed one
unpaired arrow each.

\begin{grading}
1 pt for the balanced condition, 1 pt for the two exceptions tied to the start
and the end, 1 pt for connectivity. A student who writes only ``in-degree $=$
out-degree everywhere'' has described an Euler \emph{circuit}; give 2 of the 3.
``The graph must be in one piece'' counts for connectivity, and so does
``strongly connected''.
\end{grading}

\textbf{(b) [2 pts]} One model answer. Euler path:
$A \to B \to C \to A \to D$.

\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}[scale=0.85, every node/.style={transform shape}]
  \node[gnode] (A) at (0,0) {A};
  \node[gnode] (B) at (2.2,1.2) {B};
  \node[gnode] (C) at (2.2,-1.2) {C};
  \node[gnode] (D) at (-2.2,0) {D};
  \draw[garrow] (A) -- (B);
  \draw[garrow] (B) -- (C);
  \draw[garrow] (C) -- (A);
  \draw[garrow] (A) -- (D);
  \node[glabel, below=1.5mm of A] {\textbf{S} --- out 2, in 1};
  \node[glabel, below=1.5mm of D] {\textbf{E} --- out 0, in 1};
  \node[glabel, right=1mm of B] {out 1, in 1};
  \node[glabel, right=1mm of C] {out 1, in 1};
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}

\begin{grading}
1 pt for a graph that is connected, has 4 or 5 nodes, and satisfies the degree
condition. 1 pt for marking \textbf{S} and \textbf{E} on the two nodes that
are actually unbalanced. A correct graph with the endpoints unmarked or marked
wrong keeps the first point only.
\end{grading}

\textbf{(c) [1 pt]} One model answer: three edges all leaving $A$.

\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}[scale=0.85, every node/.style={transform shape}]
  \node[gnode] (A) at (0,0) {A};
  \node[gnode] (B) at (2.2,1.2) {B};
  \node[gnode] (C) at (2.4,0) {C};
  \node[gnode] (D) at (2.2,-1.2) {D};
  \draw[garrow] (A) -- (B);
  \draw[garrow] (A) -- (C);
  \draw[garrow] (A) -- (D);
  \node[glabel, left=1mm of A] {out 3, in 0};
  \node[glabel, right=1mm of B] {out 0, in 1};
  \node[glabel, right=1mm of C] {out 0, in 1};
  \node[glabel, right=1mm of D] {out 0, in 1};
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}

$B$, $C$ and $D$ each have one more edge in than out, so each can only be an
endpoint, and a walk has one end, not three. The other family of correct
answers is \textbf{disconnected}: two separate directed triangles, where the
degree condition passes and connectivity fails.

\begin{grading}
The single point needs both halves: a graph that genuinely has no Euler path,
\emph{and} the offending node or nodes named with their in- and out-degrees. A
common wrong answer is a graph the student believes fails but which actually
has a path --- check by counting, not by trusting the label.
\end{grading}
