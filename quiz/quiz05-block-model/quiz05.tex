\question{1}{4}{Communities in pure noise.}

You generate Erd\H{o}s--R\'enyi random networks with $N = 10{,}000$ nodes:
every pair of nodes is joined with the same probability, so no communities are
planted. You make one at each average degree
$\langle k \rangle = 1, 2, 4, \dots, 128$, and on each you find the partition
with the highest modularity, $Q_{\max}$.

(a) [2 pts] As $\langle k \rangle$ grows, does $Q_{\max}$ go up, go down, or
stay flat? Sketch $Q_{\max}$ against $\langle k \rangle$ (log scale for
$\langle k \rangle$). What value does $Q_{\max}$ head toward when the network
is very sparse, and when it is complete ($\langle k \rangle = N - 1$)?

(b) [2 pts] Explain why $Q_{\max}$ moves this way. Then: a paper reports
$Q = 0.45$ for a network with $N = 10{,}000$ and $\langle k \rangle = 4$, and
calls it strong community structure. What would you check before believing
it?

\question{2}{6}{The likelihood of the degree-corrected block model.}

\begin{tcolorbox}[colback=pltblue!4, colframe=pltblue!35, boxrule=0.5pt,
                  sharp corners, boxsep=2pt, left=6pt, right=6pt,
                  top=4pt, bottom=4pt]
{\small
An undirected network has $N$ nodes and $M$ edges. $A_{ij} = A_{ji}$ is the
number of edges between nodes $i$ and $j$ ($A_{ii} = 0$), and
$k_i = \sum_j A_{ij}$ is the degree of node $i$. Node $i$ belongs to group
$c_i \in \{1, \dots, K\}$. The \emph{degree-corrected block model} draws, for
every pair $i < j$ independently,
\[
  A_{ij} \sim \mathrm{Poisson}\!\left(\theta_i \theta_j\, w_{c_i c_j}\right),
  \qquad
  \text{with} \quad \sum_{i:\,c_i = r} \theta_i = 1 \ \text{ for every group } r,
\]
where $\theta_i > 0$ and $w_{rs} = w_{sr} > 0$. Write $m_{rs}$ for the number
of edge ends in group $r$ whose other end is in group $s$ (an edge inside
group $r$ counts twice in $m_{rr}$), and
$\kappa_r = \sum_{i:\,c_i = r} k_i$ for the total degree of group $r$.

$N$ is large: you may replace $\sum_{i<j}$ by $\tfrac{1}{2}\sum_i \sum_j$, and
drop any term that depends on none of $c$, $\theta$, $w$.
}
\end{tcolorbox}

(a) [2 pts] Write the log-likelihood
$\mathcal{L}(c, \theta, w) = \log P(A \mid c, \theta, w)$. Simplify it until no
sum over pairs of nodes is left.

(b) [2 pts] Fix the assignment $c$. Find the maximum-likelihood estimates
$\hat{\theta}_i$ and $\hat{w}_{rs}$.

(c) [2 pts] Substitute them back, and write $\mathcal{L}$ as a function of the
assignment $c$ alone, in terms of $m_{rs}$ and $\kappa_r$.

\vfill

{\footnotesize\color{rulegrey}
Done early? (not graded) Without the $\theta$'s, the same steps give
$\tfrac12 \sum_{r,s} m_{rs} \log\!\big(m_{rs} / n_r n_s\big)$, where $n_r$ is
the number of nodes in group $r$. What did $\theta$ change, and why does that
stop the model from putting all the hubs in one group?}

{\large\bfseries Hints for Question 2}\par
\vspace{-0.4em}{\color{rulegrey}\rule{\textwidth}{0.6pt}}\par
\vspace{0.2em}
{\small Read only as far as you need. Each hint names a move; working it out is
still yours.}

\vspace{0.6em}

\textbf{Hint 1 --- The Poisson distribution.}
If $A \sim \mathrm{Poisson}(\lambda)$, then
\[
  P(A = a) = \frac{\lambda^{a} e^{-\lambda}}{a!} .
\]
The pairs are independent, so the likelihood is a product over pairs, and its
logarithm is a sum over pairs.

\vspace{0.6em}

\textbf{Hint 2 --- From pairs to nodes.}
A factor that depends on only one end of the pair can leave the inner sum:
\[
  \sum_i \sum_j A_{ij}\, f(i) \;=\; \sum_i f(i) \Big( \sum_j A_{ij} \Big) .
\]
What is the bracket? Since $A_{ij} = A_{ji}$, a factor $f(j)$ goes the same
way.

\vspace{0.6em}

\textbf{Hint 3 --- From pairs to groups.}
A factor that depends only on the groups of the two ends can be collected one
pair of groups at a time:
\[
  \sum_i \sum_j A_{ij}\, g(c_i, c_j)
  \;=\; \sum_r \sum_s g(r, s) \sum_{i:\,c_i = r} \ \sum_{j:\,c_j = s} A_{ij} .
\]
What does the last double sum count? Make the same move on the term that has
no $A_{ij}$ in it, and then use the normalization of $\theta$.

\vspace{0.6em}

\textbf{Hint 4 --- Maximizing under a constraint.}
The $\theta_i$ of one group must sum to 1, so you cannot just set
$\partial \mathcal{L} / \partial \theta_i = 0$. Add one Lagrange multiplier
per group and maximize
\[
  \mathcal{L} \;-\; \sum_r \mu_r \Big( \sum_{i:\,c_i = r} \theta_i - 1 \Big) .
\]
