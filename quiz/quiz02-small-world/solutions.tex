\question{1}{6}{Measure this network.}

The network has 6 nodes and 7 edges. Degrees:
$\deg A = 5$, $\deg B = \deg C = \deg E = \deg F = 2$, $\deg D = 1$.
The two triangles are drawn thick.

\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}[scale=0.78, every node/.style={transform shape}]
  \draw[gtri] (0,0) -- (0,2.1) -- (2.0,0.65) -- cycle;
  \draw[gtri] (0,0) -- (-2.0,0.65) -- (-1.23,-1.70) -- cycle;
  \node[gnode] (A) at ( 0.00,  0.00) {A};
  \node[gnode] (B) at ( 0.00,  2.10) {B};
  \node[gnode] (F) at ( 2.00,  0.65) {F};
  \node[gnode] (D) at ( 1.23, -1.70) {D};
  \node[gnode] (E) at (-1.23, -1.70) {E};
  \node[gnode] (C) at (-2.00,  0.65) {C};
  \draw[gedge] (A) -- (B); \draw[gedge] (A) -- (C); \draw[gedge] (A) -- (D);
  \draw[gedge] (A) -- (E); \draw[gedge] (A) -- (F);
  \draw[gedge] (B) -- (F); \draw[gedge] (C) -- (E);
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}

\textbf{(a) [3 pts]} \emph{Triangles:} $ABF$ and $ACE$, so \textbf{2}.

\emph{Connected triples:} a node contributes $\binom{\deg}{2}$.
\[
  \underbrace{\tbinom{5}{2}}_{A} + \underbrace{\tbinom{2}{2}}_{B}
  + \underbrace{\tbinom{2}{2}}_{C} + \underbrace{\tbinom{1}{2}}_{D}
  + \underbrace{\tbinom{2}{2}}_{E} + \underbrace{\tbinom{2}{2}}_{F}
  = 10 + 1 + 1 + 0 + 1 + 1 = \mathbf{14}.
\]
\[
  C \;=\; \frac{3 \times 2}{14} \;=\; \frac{3}{7} \;\approx\; \mathbf{0.43}.
\]

\begin{grading}
One point each for the triangle count, the triple count, and the value.
Watch for two things. \textbf{The average \emph{local} clustering coefficient
is a different number} --- $\frac{1}{6}(0.2 + 1 + 1 + 0 + 1 + 1) = 0.7$ --- and
the sheet asked for the global one; a student who computes $0.7$ correctly
keeps at most 1 point, for the per-node work. \textbf{Dropping the factor $3$}
gives $1/7 \approx 0.14$: keep both counting points, lose the third.
\end{grading}

\textbf{(b) [3 pts]} There are $\binom{6}{2} = 15$ pairs.

\emph{Distance 1} --- one pair per edge, so \textbf{7}: $AB$, $AC$, $AD$,
$AE$, $AF$, $BF$, $CE$.

\emph{Distance 2} --- the remaining \textbf{8} pairs. Every one of them is two
non-neighbours among $\{B,C,D,E,F\}$, and each is joined through $A$: $BC$,
$BD$, $BE$, $CD$, $CF$, $DE$, $DF$, $EF$. Nothing is further, because $A$ is
adjacent to everything.
\[
  \langle \ell \rangle \;=\; \frac{7 \times 1 + 8 \times 2}{15}
  \;=\; \frac{23}{15} \;\approx\; \mathbf{1.53}.
\]

\begin{grading}
One point for the split ($7$ at distance 1, $8$ at distance 2), one for the sum
$23$, one for $23/15$. Counting \emph{ordered} pairs gives $46/30$, the same
number --- full marks. Dividing by $36$ or $21$ (self-pairs included) loses the
third point only.
\end{grading}

\question{2}{4}{A tempting index.}

For the network above, $S = \dfrac{3/7}{23/15} = \dfrac{45}{161} \approx 0.28$.

\textbf{(a) [2 pts]} \emph{A case that ranks the wrong way round.} A
$1000$-node Watts--Strogatz ring at a small rewiring probability is the
textbook small world: $C \approx 0.5$, $\langle\ell\rangle \approx 5$, so
$S \approx 0.1$. The six-node network on the front of this sheet --- a star
with two extra edges, and no small-world character whatever --- scores
$S \approx 0.28$. $S$ calls the second one nearly three times the more
``small-world'' of the two.

\emph{What the case exposes.} \textbf{$S$ has no baseline, and its two halves
do not live on the same scale.} ``Small world'' is a \emph{comparative} claim:
paths as short as in a random network of the same size and density, while
clustering is far higher than that random network's. $S$ compares nothing --- it
divides a bounded quantity by an unbounded one. $C \in [0,1]$ always, while
$\langle \ell \rangle$ grows with the number of nodes, roughly like
$\log n / \log \langle k \rangle$. So $S$ falls as a network gets bigger
\emph{whatever its structure}, and two networks of different sizes cannot be
compared with it at all.

\emph{Other cases that work just as well.} The complete graph maximises $S$:
$C = 1$, $\langle\ell\rangle = 1$, $S = 1$, and nobody calls a clique a small
world --- it has no long distances to shorten. Any triangle-free network has
$C = 0$ and therefore $S = 0$, however short its paths. And
$\langle\ell\rangle$ is undefined the moment the network is disconnected, so
$S$ cannot be computed for most real data at all.

\begin{grading}
Two points.
\textbf{(i) The concrete case}, and \emph{check it} --- the sheet asked for one,
so an answer that only says ``it is not normalized'' earns nothing here. Any
of the four above is full credit: the two-network inversion, the clique, a
triangle-free network, a disconnected one. A student who asserts an inversion
without numbers, or whose numbers do not actually invert, gets 0 for this
point; name the failure in the feedback.
\textbf{(ii) The defect the case exposes} --- the missing baseline, or the
size dependence, or equivalently that a bounded numerator over an unbounded
denominator cannot be compared across networks. A case presented with no
statement of what is wrong keeps (i) only.

A student who attacks $S$ for \emph{mixing a per-triple ratio with a per-pair
mean}, or for \emph{rewarding density rather than the small-world trade-off},
has named the same defect in other words --- award (ii).
\end{grading}

\textbf{(b) [2 pts]} Normalize each half against a randomized network with the
same number of nodes and the same degree sequence (a configuration-model
rewiring), averaged over several draws:
\[
  \sigma \;=\; \frac{C / C_{\text{rand}}}{\langle\ell\rangle /
  \langle\ell\rangle_{\text{rand}}}, \qquad \sigma \gg 1
  \;\Longrightarrow\; \text{small world.}
\]

\emph{Why this fixes it.} Both halves are now ratios against the \emph{same}
network with its edges shuffled, so both are dimensionless multiples of what
chance alone would give. The growth of $\langle\ell\rangle$ with $n$ appears in
the numerator and the denominator of $\langle\ell\rangle / \langle\ell\rangle
_{\text{rand}}$ and cancels: $\sigma$ no longer drifts with size, and two
networks of different sizes become comparable. It also disposes of the clique,
which \emph{is} its own randomization and so scores $\sigma = 1$ rather than the
maximum --- what is left is exactly the Watts--Strogatz statement: much more
clustering than chance, at no cost in path length.

\begin{grading}
Two points.
\textbf{(i) The definition}, and it must name its reference. $\sigma$ is the
model answer; Telesford's
$\omega = \langle\ell\rangle_{\text{rand}}/\langle\ell\rangle - C/C_{\text{latt}}$
is equally correct, and so is any home-made index that divides through by a
randomized null. An Erd\H{o}s--R\'enyi null with the same $n$ and the same
number of edges is fine and is what $\sigma$ was first defined against.
\textbf{``Divide by a random graph'' with no statement of which random graph
earns nothing} --- the same-size, same-degrees constraint is the content.
A repair that rescales only one half (say $C/C_{\text{rand}}$ with
$\langle\ell\rangle$ dropped) also earns nothing: it stops measuring short
paths.
\textbf{(ii) Why it works.} The point is for connecting the repair back to the
defect named in (a) --- the size dependence cancels, or both halves now mean
``relative to chance'', or the student's own counterexample is worked through
and no longer breaks. A correct $\sigma$ with no explanation, or with
``because it is normalized'' and nothing more, keeps (i) only.
\end{grading}
