\question{1}{5}{The people.}

(a) [2] Normalise: $\int_1^\infty C k^{-\gamma} dk = C/(\gamma-1) = 1$, so
$C = \gamma - 1$. Then
\[
  P(k) = \int_k^\infty (\gamma-1)\,k'^{-\gamma}\,dk' = \ans{k^{-(\gamma-1)}},
  \qquad
  \log P(k) = -(\gamma-1)\log k ,
\]
a straight line of \ans{slope $1-\gamma$}. The CCDF is a power law with
exponent one smaller than the density's.
Marking: the integral from $k$ to $\infty$ set up $+1$; the result and the
slope $+1$. Skipping $C$ (writing $P(k) \propto k^{-(\gamma-1)}$) is full
marks: the slope does not depend on it. Slope $-\gamma$: $0$ of the second
point.

\begin{minipage}[t]{0.47\textwidth}
\vspace{0pt}
(b) [1] Nine points on a straight line (people column of the table above:
count the people below each row, divide by $N$; \emph{strictly} greater).
The axes are hand-drawn, in any base: they count as log--log if the powers
of the base are evenly spaced (base 2 suits these degrees best); linear axes
score $0$. The $k = 512$ row is $0$ and
cannot be plotted; no penalty for leaving it off. A plot of $P(K \ge k)$ is
shifted one step left: $-0.5$.

(c) [2] Slope $\ans{\approx -1.5}$ (accept $-1.3$ to $-1.7$), so
$\ans{\gamma = 1 - \text{slope} \approx 2.5}$ (accept $2.3$ to $2.7$).
From the table: $k$ from 2 to 128 is $1.81$ decades; the CCDF falls from
$0.177$ to $0.0003$, $2.77$ decades; $-2.77/1.81 = -1.53$.

A slope computed from two points, $\Delta\log P / \Delta\log k$, is as good
as one measured off the drawing; a hand-drawn scale with unequal decades
gives a wrong measured slope, so judge by the numbers when they are shown.
Marking (c): the slope $+1$; $\gamma$ from it $+1$.
\textbf{$\gamma = 1.5$ (slope read as $-\gamma$) scores 1 of 2}, and
contradicts the student's own answer to (a).

\question{2}{5}{The friends.}

(a) [2] A random (person, friend) pair lands on a friend of degree $k$ with
probability $k\,n_k / 2M$: each person of degree $k$ is the second entry of
$k$ pairs. So weight each row by $k$, count down from the bottom, and divide
by $2M = 26{,}228$ (friend columns of the table). The \ans{$k\,n_k$
weighting} is what this part tests; the plotted points should sit on the
key's values. Marked from the plot and the working in the photo.
\begin{itemize}[nosep, leftmargin=1.2em]
  \item Correct weighting, one or two points misplaced: $-0.5$ each.
  \item Unweighted (friend points on top of the people's): $0$ of 2, and (b) is then
        marked on its own terms only.
  \item Weighted by $k^2$, or divided by $N$ instead of $2M$: 1 of 2.
\end{itemize}
The friend line bends down over its last three points; that is correct, not
an error (see (b)).

(b) [3] Slope $\ans{\approx -0.55}$ over $k = 1$ to $32$ (accept $-0.4$ to
$-0.8$). It is \ans{shallower by about 1}. Why: the friend's degree
distribution is $q(k) = k\,p(k)/\langle k \rangle \sim k^{-(\gamma-1)}$, so
by Question 1(a) its CCDF has slope $1 - (\gamma - 1) = \ans{2 - \gamma}
= -0.5$. High-degree people are named as a friend once per friendship, so
the friend distribution carries one extra factor of $k$, which lifts the
tail.

The last three points fall off the line because nobody has more than 512
friends: the CCDF must reach 0 at $k = 512$, and the friend line, being
shallow, is still high when it has to start falling. A student who fits the
whole line gets about $-0.65$; accept it.

Marking (b): the slope $+1$; ``shallower by about 1'' $+1$; the reason
($k\,p(k)$, or ``each person counted once per friendship'') $+1$. ``Friends
have more friends'' with no link to the slope: $+0.5$ of the last point.

The friendship paradox is visible in the plot: at $k = 64$, 0.1\% of people
have more friends, but 7.3\% of friends do.
