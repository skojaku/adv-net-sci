\question{1}{5}{Your friends have more friends than you.}

\textbf{(a) [3 pts]} \emph{Count the friend list by the friend instead of by
the person.} The double sum runs over every (person, friend) pair. A given
node $j$ appears as the \emph{friend} once for each neighbor that names it,
and because $G$ is undirected, $j \in \mathcal{N}(i) \iff i \in \mathcal{N}(j)$
--- so $j$ is named by exactly $k_j$ people. Interchanging the order of
summation,
\[
  \sum_{i} \sum_{j \in \mathcal{N}(i)} k_j
  \;=\; \sum_{j} \underbrace{k_j}_{\text{times named}} \cdot\; k_j
  \;=\; \sum_{j} k_j^2 \;=\; N \langle k^2 \rangle .
\]
The denominator is the degree sum, $2M = \sum_i k_i = N \langle k \rangle > 0$.
Hence
\[
  \langle k_f \rangle \;=\; \frac{N \langle k^2 \rangle}{N \langle k \rangle}
  \;=\; \frac{\langle k^2 \rangle}{\langle k \rangle} ,
  \qquad\text{so}\qquad
  \langle k_f \rangle - \langle k \rangle
  \;=\; \frac{\langle k^2 \rangle - \langle k \rangle^2}{\langle k \rangle}
  \;=\; \frac{\mathrm{Var}(k)}{\langle k \rangle} \;\ge\; 0 ,
\]
because a variance is an average of squares and $\langle k \rangle > 0$.
Therefore $\langle k \rangle \le \langle k_f \rangle$ in \emph{every} finite
network with at least one edge. Nothing beyond undirectedness was used: this
is a counting identity, not a property of social networks.

\begin{grading}
One point each.
\textbf{(i) The counting step} --- node $j$ is named $k_j$ times, giving
$\sum_i \sum_{j \in \mathcal{N}(i)} k_j = \sum_j k_j^2$. Equivalently, a
student may say that picking a (person, friend) pair is picking an edge
endpoint, write $q(k) = k\,p(k)/\langle k \rangle$ and then
$\langle k_f \rangle = \sum_k k\,q(k)$; that is the same argument and earns the
point. ``Hubs get counted more often'' \emph{with no algebra} earns nothing
here.
\textbf{(ii) The ratio} --- the denominator $2M = N\langle k \rangle$, and
therefore $\langle k_f \rangle = \langle k^2 \rangle/\langle k \rangle$. The
sheet did not print this; producing it is the point.
\textbf{(iii) The sign} --- rewriting the gap as
$\mathrm{Var}(k)/\langle k \rangle$ and concluding $\ge 0$ because a variance
is never negative. A student who only asserts $\langle k^2\rangle \ge \langle
k\rangle^2$ without a reason keeps the point \emph{if} they name the variance
or cite Cauchy--Schwarz / Jensen; a bare assertion does not.

\textbf{Cauchy--Schwarz is a full-credit alternative} for (iii):
$(\sum_i k_i)^2 \le N \sum_i k_i^2$ gives $\langle k \rangle^2 \le \langle k^2
\rangle$ directly. $\langle k \rangle > 0$ is worth a mention but not a point.
\end{grading}

\textbf{(b) [2 pts]} Equality holds \textbf{exactly when every node has the
same degree} --- when $G$ is $r$-regular for some $r \ge 1$.

\emph{($\Leftarrow$)} If $k_i = r$ for all $i$ then $\langle k \rangle = r$ and
$\langle k^2 \rangle = r^2$, so
$\langle k_f \rangle = r^2 / r = r = \langle k \rangle$.

\emph{($\Rightarrow$)} If $\langle k_f \rangle = \langle k \rangle$ then by (a)
$\mathrm{Var}(k) = 0$. Since
$\mathrm{Var}(k) = \frac{1}{N}\sum_i (k_i - \langle k \rangle)^2$ is a sum of
non-negative terms, every term must vanish, so $k_i = \langle k \rangle$ for
every $i$. (A ring, a complete graph $K_n$, or any other regular network is an
example; $r \ge 1$ rather than $r \ge 0$ only because $M \ge 1$ rules out the
empty network.)

\begin{grading}
One point each.
\textbf{(i) The condition} --- ``all degrees equal'', ``regular network'', or
``$\mathrm{Var}(k) = 0$'' are all the same answer and all count. An example
network is not required, but a student who offers one that is \emph{not}
regular has contradicted their own condition: lose half.
\textbf{(ii) Both directions.} One direction only is half. The
($\Rightarrow$) direction must say why $\mathrm{Var}(k) = 0$ forces every
degree to equal the mean --- ``a sum of squares vanishes only term by term'';
restating $\mathrm{Var}(k) = 0$ is not a proof of it.

\textbf{Wrong conditions to watch for:} ``when the network is connected'',
``when it is a random / Erd\H{o}s--R\'enyi network'', ``when it is a tree'',
``when there are no hubs''. All score 0 for (i). A Poisson network is
\emph{not} regular --- its variance equals its mean.
\end{grading}

\question{2}{5}{How much can you delete before it falls apart?}

\textbf{(a) [3 pts]} \emph{Step 1: the degree at the end of a random edge.}
Cut every edge in half: there are $\sum_i k_i = N \langle k \rangle$
half-edges, and following a random edge is following a random half-edge. The
$N p(k)$ nodes of degree $k$ own $k$ half-edges each, so
\[
  q(k) \;=\; \frac{N\,p(k) \cdot k}{N \langle k \rangle}
  \;=\; \frac{k\,p(k)}{\langle k \rangle},
  \qquad
  \sum_k k\, q(k) \;=\; \frac{1}{\langle k \rangle}\sum_k k^2 p(k)
  \;=\; \frac{\langle k^2 \rangle}{\langle k \rangle} \;=\; \kappa .
\]

\emph{Step 2: the branching factor and the criterion.} One of those $\kappa$
edges is the one we arrived on, so the expected number of \emph{onward} edges
is $\kappa - 1$. The network is locally tree-like, so the outward exploration
is a branching process with that mean offspring: below $1$ each shell is
smaller than the last and the exploration dies out, leaving only finite
components; above $1$ the shells grow geometrically and reach a finite fraction
of the $N$ nodes. Hence a giant component exists iff $\kappa - 1 > 1$, i.e.
\[
  \kappa = \frac{\langle k^2 \rangle}{\langle k \rangle} > 2
  \qquad \text{(Molloy--Reed)} .
\]

\emph{Step 3: dilute and solve.} With each node surviving independently with
probability $1-f$, every onward edge leads to a survivor with that
probability, and the survivors are still wired at random given their degrees.
The branching factor is thinned to $(1-f)(\kappa - 1)$, and the giant
component dies when it falls to $1$:
\[
  (1-f_c)(\kappa - 1) = 1
  \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
  \boxed{\;f_c = 1 - \frac{1}{\kappa - 1}\;}
\]
\emph{The same answer the long way.} A survivor of original degree $k_0$ keeps
$\mathrm{Binomial}(k_0, 1-f)$ edges, so with $p = 1-f$,
$\langle k \rangle_p = p \langle k \rangle_0$ and
$\langle k^2 \rangle_p = p^2 \langle k^2 \rangle_0 + p(1-p)\langle k \rangle_0$,
whence $\kappa_p = p\,\kappa_0 + (1-p)$. Setting $\kappa_p = 2$ gives
$p_c = 1/(\kappa_0 - 1)$ and $f_c = 1 - p_c$, as above.

\begin{grading}
One point per step, halves allowed.
\textbf{(i) $q(k)$.} The half-edge count is the content: degree-$k$ nodes own
$k$ of the $N\langle k \rangle$ endpoints each. Quoting
$q(k) = k p(k)/\langle k \rangle$ with no derivation earns 0.
\textbf{(ii) $\kappa - 1$ and $\kappa > 2$}, half each. The $-1$ is the edge
arrived on and must be justified, not merely written; the criterion needs its
branching-process reason. $\mathbb{E}[k(k-2)] > 0$ is the same criterion ---
full credit.
\textbf{(iii) The dilution and the solve}, half each: the factor $(1-f)$ on
the branching factor (or the $\kappa_p = p\kappa_0 + (1-p)$ computation), and
$f_c = 1 - 1/(\kappa - 1)$ with the threshold set at branching factor $= 1$
(equivalently $\kappa_p = 2$). A student who deletes \emph{edges} rather than
nodes and says so still earns the dilution half; the threshold is the same.

\textbf{The three near-misses.}
$f_c = 1 - 1/\kappa$ --- the incoming edge was forgotten; lose the solve half.
Reporting $1/(\kappa-1)$ as the answer --- that is the fraction that
\emph{remains}; award the solve half only if the student says which is which.
Setting the branching factor to $0$ --- the exploration dies at $1$, not $0$;
lose the solve half.

\textbf{$\langle k \rangle > 1$ is not the criterion.} That is the
Erd\H{o}s--R\'enyi condition, which coincides with $\kappa > 2$ only for a
Poisson $p(k)$. Credit it only if the student restricts it to that case.
\end{grading}

\textbf{(b) [2 pts]}
\textbf{(i) Poisson.} A Poisson distribution has variance equal to its mean,
so $\langle k^2 \rangle = \langle k \rangle^2 + \langle k \rangle$ and
$\kappa = \langle k \rangle + 1$. Hence
\[
  f_c = 1 - \frac{1}{(\langle k \rangle + 1) - 1}
      = 1 - \frac{1}{\langle k \rangle} .
\]
Robustness is set by the average degree alone, and $f_c < 1$ always: a random
network can always be shattered by removing enough nodes at random.

\textbf{(ii) $2 < \gamma < 3$.} $\langle k^2 \rangle$ diverges, so
$\kappa \to \infty$ and $f_c \to 1$. Essentially every node must fail before
the giant component dies --- such a network is almost immune to random
failure. (It is not immune to a \emph{targeted} attack, where the same hubs
are the weakness; that is the robust-yet-fragile paradox.)

\begin{grading}
One point each.
\textbf{(i)} $\kappa = \langle k \rangle + 1$ and
$f_c = 1 - 1/\langle k \rangle$. \textbf{The sheet no longer prints the
Poisson second moment}, so supplying $\langle k^2 \rangle = \langle k \rangle^2
+ \langle k \rangle$ is part of the point; expect this to be where the question
is lost.
\textbf{Watch for $\langle k^2 \rangle = \langle k \rangle^2$}, which gives
$\kappa = \langle k \rangle$ and $f_c = 1 - 1/(\langle k \rangle - 1)$. That is
the zero-variance \emph{regular} case --- no credit, and say so in the
feedback, because it is the same confusion as Question 1(b).
\textbf{(ii)} $f_c \to 1$, \emph{with} one sentence of meaning (``almost every
node must be removed'', ``extremely robust to random failure''). The limit
alone is half.

\textbf{Watch for ``it can never break''.} $f_c \to 1$ is a statement about
$N \to \infty$; a finite network has a finite largest hub and so $f_c < 1$. Do
not deduct if the limit and its reading are otherwise right --- note it in the
feedback.
\end{grading}
