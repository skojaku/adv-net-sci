\question{1}{5}{Your friends have more friends than you.}

\textbf{(a) [3 pts]} \emph{Count the friend list by the friend instead of by
the person.} The double sum runs over every (person, friend) pair. A given
node $j$ appears as the \emph{friend} once for each neighbor that names it,
and because $G$ is undirected, $j \in \mathcal{N}(i) \iff i \in \mathcal{N}(j)$
--- so $j$ is named by exactly $k_j$ people. Interchanging the order of
summation,
\[
  \sum_{i} \sum_{j \in \mathcal{N}(i)} k_j
  \;=\; \sum_{j} \underbrace{k_j}_{\text{times named}} \cdot\; k_j
  \;=\; \sum_{j} k_j^2 \;=\; N \langle k^2 \rangle .
\]
The denominator is the degree sum, $2M = \sum_i k_i = N \langle k \rangle$.
Hence
\[
  \langle k_f \rangle \;=\; \frac{N \langle k^2 \rangle}{N \langle k \rangle}
  \;=\; \frac{\langle k^2 \rangle}{\langle k \rangle} .
\]
Dividing needs $\langle k \rangle > 0$, which is exactly what $M \ge 1$
guarantees. Subtracting $\langle k \rangle$ and using
$\mathrm{Var}(k) = \langle k^2 \rangle - \langle k \rangle^2$,
\[
  \langle k_f \rangle - \langle k \rangle
  \;=\; \frac{\langle k^2 \rangle - \langle k \rangle^2}{\langle k \rangle}
  \;=\; \frac{\mathrm{Var}(k)}{\langle k \rangle} \;\ge\; 0 ,
\]
because a variance is an average of squares and $\langle k \rangle > 0$.
Therefore $\langle k \rangle \le \langle k_f \rangle$ in \emph{every} finite
network with at least one edge. Nothing beyond undirectedness was used: this
is a counting identity, not a property of social networks.

\begin{grading}
One point each.
\textbf{(i) The counting step} --- node $j$ is named $k_j$ times, giving
$\sum_i \sum_{j \in \mathcal{N}(i)} k_j = \sum_j k_j^2$. Equivalently, a
student may say that picking a (person, friend) pair is picking an edge
endpoint, write $q(k) = k\,p(k)/\langle k \rangle$ and then
$\langle k_f \rangle = \sum_k k\,q(k)$; that is the same argument and earns the
point. ``Hubs get counted more often'' \emph{with no algebra} earns nothing
here.
\textbf{(ii) Assembling the ratio} --- the denominator $2M = N\langle k
\rangle$, and $\langle k_f \rangle = \langle k^2 \rangle/\langle k \rangle$.
\textbf{(iii) The sign} --- rewriting the gap as
$\mathrm{Var}(k)/\langle k \rangle$ and concluding $\ge 0$ because a variance
is never negative. A student who only asserts $\langle k^2\rangle \ge \langle
k\rangle^2$ without a reason keeps the point \emph{if} they name the variance
or cite Cauchy--Schwarz / Jensen; a bare assertion does not.

\textbf{Cauchy--Schwarz is a full-credit alternative} for (iii):
$(\sum_i k_i)^2 \le N \sum_i k_i^2$ gives $\langle k \rangle^2 \le \langle k^2
\rangle$ directly. Mentioning $\langle k \rangle > 0$ is expected but its
absence costs nothing if the rest is right.
\end{grading}

\textbf{(b) [2 pts]} Equality holds \textbf{exactly when every node has the
same degree} --- when $G$ is $r$-regular for some $r \ge 1$.

\emph{($\Leftarrow$)} If $k_i = r$ for all $i$ then $\langle k \rangle = r$ and
$\langle k^2 \rangle = r^2$, so
$\langle k_f \rangle = r^2 / r = r = \langle k \rangle$.

\emph{($\Rightarrow$)} If $\langle k_f \rangle = \langle k \rangle$ then by (a)
$\mathrm{Var}(k) = 0$. Since
$\mathrm{Var}(k) = \frac{1}{N}\sum_i (k_i - \langle k \rangle)^2$ is a sum of
non-negative terms, every term must vanish, so $k_i = \langle k \rangle$ for
every $i$.

\emph{An example.} The 4-cycle $C_4$ (every degree 2). Any ring, any complete
graph $K_n$, or any other regular network is equally good.
($r \ge 1$ rather than $r \ge 0$ only because $M \ge 1$ rules out the empty
network.)

\begin{grading}
One point each.
\textbf{(i) The condition and an example} --- ``all degrees equal'', ``regular
network'', or ``$\mathrm{Var}(k) = 0$'' are all the same answer and all count,
provided a valid regular example of $\ge 4$ nodes is named. A correct condition
with a \emph{non-regular} example (or no example) keeps half.
\textbf{(ii) Both directions.} One direction only is half. The
($\Rightarrow$) direction must say why $\mathrm{Var}(k) = 0$ forces every
degree to equal the mean --- ``a sum of squares vanishes only term by term'';
restating $\mathrm{Var}(k) = 0$ is not a proof of it.

\textbf{Wrong conditions to watch for:} ``when the network is connected'',
``when it is a random / Erd\H{o}s--R\'enyi network'', ``when it is a tree'',
``when there are no hubs''. All score 0 for (i). A Poisson network is
\emph{not} regular --- its variance equals its mean.
\end{grading}

\question{2}{5}{How much can you delete before it falls apart?}

\textbf{(a) [2 pts]} \emph{The degree at the end of a random edge.} Cut every
edge in half: there are $\sum_i k_i = N \langle k \rangle$ half-edges, and
picking a random edge and then one of its two ends is the same as picking one
half-edge uniformly at random. The $N p(k)$ nodes of degree $k$ own $k$
half-edges each, so
\[
  q(k) \;=\; \frac{N\,p(k) \cdot k}{N \langle k \rangle}
  \;=\; \frac{k\,p(k)}{\langle k \rangle},
  \qquad \sum_k q(k) = \frac{\langle k \rangle}{\langle k \rangle} = 1 .
\]
The node reached therefore has expected degree
\[
  \sum_k k\, q(k) \;=\; \frac{1}{\langle k \rangle}\sum_k k^2 p(k)
  \;=\; \frac{\langle k^2 \rangle}{\langle k \rangle} \;=\; \kappa ,
\]
and one of those $\kappa$ edges is the one we arrived on, so the expected
number of \emph{onward} edges --- the branching factor --- is $\kappa - 1$.

\emph{The criterion.} A giant component exists iff
\[
  \kappa - 1 > 1
  \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad
  \boxed{\;\kappa = \frac{\langle k^2 \rangle}{\langle k \rangle} > 2\;}
\]
--- the Molloy--Reed criterion. \emph{Why:} the network is locally tree-like,
so the outward exploration is a branching process with mean offspring
$\kappa - 1$; below $1$ each shell is smaller than the last and the exploration
dies out after a few steps, leaving only finite components, while above $1$ the
shells grow geometrically and reach a finite fraction of the $N$ nodes.

\begin{grading}
One point each, halves allowed.
\textbf{(i) $q(k)$, derived.} The half-edge count is the content: degree-$k$
nodes own $k$ of the $N\langle k \rangle$ endpoints each. Quoting
$q(k) = k p(k)/\langle k \rangle$ with no derivation earns 0 --- the sheet said
``show''.
\textbf{(ii) $\kappa - 1$ and $\kappa > 2$.} Half for the branching factor
$\sum_k k q(k) - 1 = \kappa - 1$ (the $-1$ is the incoming edge and must be
justified, not merely written), half for the criterion $\kappa > 2$ with a
branching-process reason. $\mathbb{E}[k(k-2)] > 0$ is the same criterion ---
full credit.

\textbf{$\langle k \rangle > 1$ is not the answer.} That is the
Erd\H{o}s--R\'enyi condition, which coincides with $\kappa > 2$ only for a
Poisson $p(k)$. Give it credit only if the student restricts it to that case.
\end{grading}

\textbf{(b) [2 pts]} Write $p = 1 - f$ for the surviving fraction. Each onward
edge now leads to a node that survives with probability $p$, independently, and
the surviving network is still wired at random given its degrees --- so the
criterion still applies, with the branching factor thinned to
\[
  p\,(\kappa - 1) \;=\; (1-f)(\kappa - 1).
\]
The giant component disappears when that falls to $1$:
\[
  (1-f_c)(\kappa - 1) = 1
  \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
  1 - f_c = \frac{1}{\kappa - 1}
  \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
  \boxed{\;f_c = 1 - \frac{1}{\kappa - 1}\;}
\]
\emph{The same answer the long way.} A survivor of original degree $k_0$ keeps
$\mathrm{Binomial}(k_0, p)$ edges, so
$\langle k \rangle_p = p \langle k \rangle_0$ and
$\langle k^2 \rangle_p = p^2 \langle k^2 \rangle_0 + p(1-p)\langle k \rangle_0$,
whence $\kappa_p = p\,\kappa_0 + (1-p)$. Setting $\kappa_p = 2$ gives
$p_c = 1/(\kappa_0 - 1)$ and $f_c = 1 - p_c$, as above.

\begin{grading}
One point each, halves allowed.
\textbf{(i) The dilution.} The branching factor picks up a factor $(1-f)$,
because each onward neighbor survives independently with that probability ---
or, equivalently, the $\kappa_p = p\kappa_0 + (1-p)$ computation. A student who
deletes \emph{edges} rather than nodes and says so still earns this point; the
threshold is the same.
\textbf{(ii) Solving.} $f_c = 1 - 1/(\kappa - 1)$, with the threshold set at
branching factor $=1$ (equivalently $\kappa_p = 2$).

\textbf{The three near-misses.}
$f_c = 1 - 1/\kappa$ --- the incoming edge was forgotten; (i) stands, (ii) is
lost.
Reporting $1/(\kappa-1)$ as the answer --- that is the fraction that
\emph{remains}; award (ii) only if the student says which is which.
Setting the branching factor to $0$ --- the exploration dies at $1$, not $0$;
(ii) is lost.
\end{grading}

\textbf{(c) [1 pt]}
\textbf{(i) Poisson.} $\kappa = (\langle k \rangle^2 + \langle k \rangle) /
\langle k \rangle = \langle k \rangle + 1$, so
\[
  f_c = 1 - \frac{1}{(\langle k \rangle + 1) - 1}
      = 1 - \frac{1}{\langle k \rangle} .
\]
Robustness is set by the average degree alone, and $f_c < 1$ always: a random
network can always be shattered by removing enough nodes at random.

\textbf{(ii) $2 < \gamma < 3$.} $\langle k^2 \rangle \to \infty$, so
$\kappa \to \infty$ and $f_c \to 1$. Essentially every node must fail before
the giant component dies --- such a network is almost immune to random failure.
(It is not immune to a \emph{targeted} attack, where the same hubs are the
weakness; that is the robust-yet-fragile paradox.)

\begin{grading}
Half each.
$f_c = 1 - 1/\langle k \rangle$ for Poisson, and $f_c \to 1$ for
$2 < \gamma < 3$ with one sentence of meaning (``almost every node must be
removed'', ``extremely robust to random failure''). The second half needs both
the limit and the sentence; the limit alone does not earn it.

\textbf{Watch for $\langle k^2 \rangle = \langle k \rangle^2$}, which gives
$\kappa = \langle k \rangle$ and $f_c = 1 - 1/(\langle k \rangle - 1)$. That is
the zero-variance \emph{regular} case, and the sheet printed the correct
Poisson second moment --- no credit for (i).

\textbf{Watch for ``it can never break''} in (ii). $f_c \to 1$ is a statement
about $N \to \infty$; a finite network has a finite largest hub and so
$f_c < 1$. The claim as stated is not right, but do not deduct for it here if
the limit and its one-sentence reading are correct --- note it in the feedback.
\end{grading}
