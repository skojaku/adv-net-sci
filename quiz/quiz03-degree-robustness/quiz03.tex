{\small
\textbf{Notation, used by both questions.}
$G$ is a finite, simple, undirected network with $N$ nodes and $M \ge 1$ edges
(no self-loops, no repeated edges). Node $i$ has \textbf{degree} $k_i$, the
number of edges touching it, and $\mathcal{N}(i)$ is its set of neighbors, so
$|\mathcal{N}(i)| = k_i$. The \textbf{degree distribution} $p(k)$ is the
fraction of nodes of degree exactly $k$; the ratio $\kappa$ below is the
\textbf{degree heterogeneity}. Averages over \emph{nodes} are
\[
  \langle k \rangle = \sum_k k\, p(k) = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i} k_i ,
  \quad
  \langle k^2 \rangle = \sum_k k^2 p(k) = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i} k_i^2 ,
  \quad
  \mathrm{Var}(k) = \langle k^2 \rangle - \langle k \rangle^2 ,
  \quad
  \kappa \;\equiv\; \frac{\langle k^2 \rangle}{\langle k \rangle} .
\]
}
\end{tcolorbox}

% ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
\question{1}{5}{Your friends have more friends than you.}

List every \textbf{(person, friend) pair} in $G$: for each node $i$ and each
$j \in \mathcal{N}(i)$, write down $(i,j)$. There are $\sum_i k_i = 2M$ such
pairs, one per edge endpoint. The \textbf{average degree of a friend} is the
friend's degree averaged over that list,
\[
  \langle k_f \rangle
  \;\equiv\; \frac{1}{2M} \sum_{i} \sum_{j \in \mathcal{N}(i)} k_j .
\]

\textbf{(a) [3 pts]} Prove the first equality below, and deduce the rest:
\[
  \langle k_f \rangle = \frac{\langle k^2 \rangle}{\langle k \rangle} ,
  \qquad\text{hence}\qquad
  \langle k_f \rangle - \langle k \rangle
  = \frac{\mathrm{Var}(k)}{\langle k \rangle} \;\ge\; 0 ,
  \qquad\text{so}\qquad
  \langle k \rangle \le \langle k_f \rangle .
\]
Note where the argument needs $\langle k \rangle > 0$.

\textbf{(b) [2 pts]} State exactly which networks satisfy
$\langle k \rangle = \langle k_f \rangle$, prove your condition in
\emph{both} directions, and name one such network with at least 4 nodes.

% ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
\question{2}{5}{How much can you delete before it falls apart?}

Now let $G$ be a \textbf{configuration model} network: $N$ nodes are given
degrees from $p(k)$, each is cut into that many half-edges, and the half-edges
are paired uniformly at random. For large $N$ it is \textbf{locally tree-like}
(short cycles are vanishingly rare), so exploring outward from a node behaves
like a branching process --- \emph{you may assume this}. A \textbf{giant
component} holds a non-vanishing fraction of the $N$ nodes as $N \to \infty$;
under \textbf{random failure} every node is deleted independently with
probability $f$, so a fraction $1-f$ survives.

\textbf{(a) [2 pts]} Pick an edge of $G$ uniformly at random and follow it to
one of its two ends. Show that the node you arrive at has degree $k$ with
probability
\[
  q(k) \;=\; \frac{k\, p(k)}{\langle k \rangle} ,
\]
and hence that the expected number of \emph{further} edges leading onward ---
the \textbf{branching factor} --- is $\kappa - 1$. Then give the condition on
$\kappa$ for a giant component to exist, with a one-sentence reason.

\textbf{(b) [2 pts]} Now delete a fraction $f$ of the nodes at random. Say what
this does to the branching factor, and solve for the \textbf{critical fraction}
$f_c$ at which the giant component disappears. Give $f_c$ in terms of $\kappa$.

\textbf{(c) [1 pt]} Evaluate $f_c$ for \textbf{(i)} a Poisson $p(k)$ of mean
$\langle k \rangle$, where
$\langle k^2 \rangle = \langle k \rangle^2 + \langle k \rangle$, and
\textbf{(ii)} $p(k) \sim k^{-\gamma}$ with $2 < \gamma < 3$ as
$N \to \infty$, where $\langle k^2 \rangle$ diverges. One sentence on what
(ii) says about such a network.
